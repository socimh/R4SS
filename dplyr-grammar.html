3  Select columns

Suppose we want to compare countries’ life expectancy and water coverage. The source has six variables, but we can make a more convenient table containing the three columns we need. Selecting columns keeps the same observations; moving columns changes their reading order.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to select columns by name, position, or name pattern; move them with relocate(); and save a result while preserving its source. We will use lifeexp, a country dataset included with tidystata. Load the packages in your current session:

library(tidyverse)
library(tidystata)

Typing the dataset name prints the table, just as in Chapter 2:

lifeexp
# A tibble: 68 × 6
       region country                popgrowth  lexp gnppc safewater
   <hvn_lbll> <chr>                      <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>     <dbl>
 1          1 Albania                     -0.6    74   870        49
 2          1 Armenia                     -1.2    70   570        83
 3          1 Austria                      0.1    78 27810        97
 4          1 Azerbaijan                   0.9    64   510        49
 5          1 Belarus                     -0.5    68  1540        82
 6          1 Belgium                      0.2    77 26530       100
 7          1 Bosnia and Herzegovina       1.8    74  1230        87
 8          1 Bulgaria                    -0.7    71  1440        90
 9          1 Croatia                     -0.1    72    NA        85
10          1 Czechia                     -0.1    75  6140        95
# ℹ 58 more rows

The header reports 68 countries and six variables. Each row describes one country, and each column records one attribute. country identifies countries; region contains labelled region codes; popgrowth measures annual population growth in percent; lexp is life expectancy at birth in years; gnppc is income per person in current US dollars; and safewater is safe-water access in percent. NA marks an unavailable measurement. Most indicators refer to 1998; safe water refers to 2000. The dataset notes document the sources and units.

We can begin by choosing columns from this table. Later, Chapter 7 examines its individual records and variable descriptions more closely.

3.1 Keep the columns you need

select() takes the data first, followed by the columns to retain. In this function, write column names directly, without quotation marks. Save the result as country_view, then type its name to inspect it:

country_view <- select(lifeexp, country, lexp, safewater)
country_view
# A tibble: 68 × 3
   country                 lexp safewater
   <chr>                  <dbl>     <dbl>
 1 Albania                   74        49
 2 Armenia                   70        83
 3 Austria                   78        97
 4 Azerbaijan                64        49
 5 Belarus                   68        82
 6 Belgium                   77       100
 7 Bosnia and Herzegovina    74        87
 8 Bulgaria                  71        90
 9 Croatia                   72        85
10 Czechia                   75        95
# ℹ 58 more rows

country_view contains all 68 countries and three columns, in the order requested. Values, missing measurements, and row order are unchanged. <- stores this result under a new name. lifeexp still has all six columns. Selection does not erase columns from the original object.

The first argument is a table name; the remaining arguments identify its columns. country identifies observations, lexp measures life expectancy in years, and safewater measures water coverage in percent. Choosing fewer variables is different from choosing fewer countries.

We can check the number of observations directly. nrow() takes a table and returns its row count:

nrow(country_view)
[1] 68

The result is 68: all countries remain in the selected table. To count its columns, use ncol():

ncol(country_view)
[1] 3

The result is 3, matching the three names supplied to select(). These counting functions leave the table unchanged.

We can request a different reading order simply by changing the order of the selected names:

select(lifeexp, country, safewater, lexp)
# A tibble: 68 × 3
   country                safewater  lexp
   <chr>                      <dbl> <dbl>
 1 Albania                       49    74
 2 Armenia                       83    70
 3 Austria                       97    78
 4 Azerbaijan                    49    64
 5 Belarus                       82    68
 6 Belgium                      100    77
 7 Bosnia and Herzegovina        87    74
 8 Bulgaria                      90    71
 9 Croatia                       85    72
10 Czechia                       95    75
# ℹ 58 more rows

Water coverage now precedes life expectancy. This command prints a result but does not replace country_view or lifeexp. Assign a result when you want to use it again.

3.2 Check column names

Sometimes we want to check the column names without reading the country records. Tidystata’s ds() takes a table and returns its column names as text, in their current order:

ds(lifeexp)
[1] "region"    "country"   "popgrowth" "lexp"      "gnppc"     "safewater"

The six names follow the order in the original table. Quotation marks show that the result contains text. select() returns a table containing data; ds() returns the names of its columns. Neither call changes the source unless we assign its result back to that name. We will reuse ds() to check column order below.

3.3 Tidyselect: ways to choose columns

Tidyselect is the shared selection language used inside select(), relocate(), and other functions that choose columns. It combines column names, operators, and helper functions. You do not need another package-loading command: the tidyverse setup above makes these helpers available.

We will first use : for a consecutive range and - to exclude columns. Then we will work through three helpers for matching text in column names, one at a time. Each method identifies columns; it does not select observations.

3.4 Select ranges and exclude columns

Whole-number positions identify columns in their current order. A range made with : includes both endpoints and every column between them:

The parentheses make region:popgrowth one selection to exclude. The result keeps lexp, gnppc, and safewater, still with 68 countries. The range includes country, so this particular demonstration also removes the identifier.

3.5 Select by name patterns

When column names share some text, a helper can spare us from typing each name. starts_with() matches text at the beginning of a column name. Supply that text in quotation marks inside the helper:

select(lifeexp, starts_with("pop"))
# A tibble: 68 × 1
   popgrowth
       <dbl>
 1      -0.6
 2      -1.2
 3       0.1
 4       0.9
 5      -0.5
 6       0.2
 7       1.8
 8      -0.7
 9      -0.1
10      -0.1
# ℹ 58 more rows

This selects popgrowth, whose name begins with pop. The result still contains all 68 countries.

To match the end of a name, use ends_with():

select(lifeexp, ends_with("water"))
# A tibble: 68 × 1
   safewater
       <dbl>
 1        49
 2        83
 3        97
 4        49
 5        82
 6       100
 7        87
 8        90
 9        85
10        95
# ℹ 58 more rows

This selects safewater, whose name ends in water. Again, only the set of columns changes.

To match text anywhere in a name, use contains():

select(lifeexp, contains("p"))
# A tibble: 68 × 3
   popgrowth  lexp gnppc
       <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
 1      -0.6    74   870
 2      -1.2    70   570
 3       0.1    78 27810
 4       0.9    64   510
 5      -0.5    68  1540
 6       0.2    77 26530
 7       1.8    74  1230
 8      -0.7    71  1440
 9      -0.1    72    NA
10      -0.1    75  6140
# ℹ 58 more rows

This selects popgrowth, lexp, and gnppc, because each name contains the letter p. All 68 countries remain. The three helpers ignore letter case by default. They match spelling, not meaning: the three names containing p measure different things.

We can combine a named identifier with a helper, or exclude the helper’s selection:

select(lifeexp, country, starts_with("pop"))
# A tibble: 68 × 2
   country                popgrowth
   <chr>                      <dbl>
 1 Albania                     -0.6
 2 Armenia                     -1.2
 3 Austria                      0.1
 4 Azerbaijan                   0.9
 5 Belarus                     -0.5
 6 Belgium                      0.2
 7 Bosnia and Herzegovina       1.8
 8 Bulgaria                    -0.7
 9 Croatia                     -0.1
10 Czechia                     -0.1
# ℹ 58 more rows

select(lifeexp, -contains("p"))
# A tibble: 68 × 3
       region country                safewater
   <hvn_lbll> <chr>                      <dbl>
 1          1 Albania                       49
 2          1 Armenia                       83
 3          1 Austria                       97
 4          1 Azerbaijan                    49
 5          1 Belarus                       82
 6          1 Belgium                      100
 7          1 Bosnia and Herzegovina        87
 8          1 Bulgaria                      90
 9          1 Croatia                       85
10          1 Czechia                       95
# ℹ 58 more rows

The first table pairs country names with population growth. The second keeps region, country, and safewater. The minus sign applies to all columns identified by the helper. Neither call changes lifeexp.

An exact name is a request for a particular variable. If we write select(lifeexp, income), R reports that income does not exist; the income column here is gnppc. A pattern can legitimately match no names. For example:

select(lifeexp, starts_with("income"))
# A tibble: 68 × 0

This returns a table with 68 rows and zero columns, without an error. No names begin with income; the helper does not know that we meant income per person. A successful call can therefore still be unsuitable for our question. Check the header and the selected names, then correct the spelling or selection rule.

A shared spelling need not identify a shared measurement. Check the selected names before interpreting the result; choosing columns is a research decision as well as a coding operation.

3.6 Move a column without dropping others

relocate() also takes the table first, but moves named columns while keeping the rest. Its default places the selected column at the front. Use the familiar ds() check to compare the result with the original:

country_front <- relocate(lifeexp, safewater)
ds(country_front)
[1] "safewater" "region"    "country"   "popgrowth" "lexp"      "gnppc"    
ds(lifeexp)
[1] "region"    "country"   "popgrowth" "lexp"      "gnppc"     "safewater"

All six columns remain. Water coverage is first in country_front; the original names show that lifeexp has not changed. No country has moved to another row.

Use .after or .before to choose a more precise location. These are argument names, including their leading dots:

country_reading <- relocate(lifeexp, safewater, .after = country)
ds(country_reading)
[1] "region"    "country"   "safewater" "popgrowth" "lexp"      "gnppc"    
relocate(country_view, safewater, .before = lexp)
# A tibble: 68 × 3
   country                safewater  lexp
   <chr>                      <dbl> <dbl>
 1 Albania                       49    74
 2 Armenia                       83    70
 3 Austria                       97    78
 4 Azerbaijan                    49    64
 5 Belarus                       82    68
 6 Belgium                      100    77
 7 Bosnia and Herzegovina        87    74
 8 Bulgaria                      90    71
 9 Croatia                       85    72
10 Czechia                       95    75
# ℹ 58 more rows

The first result puts water coverage immediately after the country name. The second puts it immediately before life expectancy in the smaller table. = supplies a named argument here; <- stores a returned result.

Multiple names in relocate() move together: relocate(lifeexp, country, lexp) puts those two columns first and keeps the others in their relative order. Ranges depend on column order:

country_reordered <- relocate(lifeexp, country, .after = safewater)
select(country_reordered, region:popgrowth)
# A tibble: 68 × 2
       region popgrowth
   <hvn_lbll>     <dbl>
 1          1      -0.6
 2          1      -1.2
 3          1       0.1
 4          1       0.9
 5          1      -0.5
 6          1       0.2
 7          1       1.8
 8          1      -0.7
 9          1      -0.1
10          1      -0.1
# ℹ 58 more rows

Now only region and popgrowth lie in that range. The country names still exist in country_reordered, but at the end. Check the column order before relying on a range.

3.7 Review

  • select() keeps columns by name, range, or position; exclusions remove columns without removing observations.
  • starts_with(), ends_with(), and contains() match literal parts of column names.
  • Name patterns match spelling rather than meaning; check that the selected columns answer the research question.
  • relocate() moves columns while retaining the others; .before and .after specify their destination.
  • ds(), nrow(), and ncol() check the result; use <- to save it without replacing the source.
