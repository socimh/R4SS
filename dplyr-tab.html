8  Tabulate data

TipIn this chapter
  • Count categories and numeric values, and distinguish separate distributions from joint and pairwise tables.
  • Combine one-way tables and inspect long results without discarding rows.
  • Distinguish percentages of all cases from percentages of valid answers, and identify the denominator within each comparison group.
  • Describe educational qualifications in the 2024 General Social Survey.

How common is a response, and how does its distribution differ across groups? A frequency table describes the distribution of categories, such as happiness or educational qualifications. It can also count each distinct recorded value of a numeric variable. Every percentage requires us to be clear about who contributes to the calculation.

tab() counts cases and reports percentages and cumulative percentages, tab1() produces a separate distribution for each selected variable, and tab2() cross-tabulates each pair of selected variables. fre() adds total rows to a frequency table.

These tabulation functions come from tidystata and return new tables without changing the data we give them.

The examples build on inspecting a dataset in Chapter 7. We supply the dataset as the first argument, followed by column names without quotation marks: for example, tab(gss24, happy). Commas separate the arguments inside the parentheses. The pipe learned in Chapter 5 gives an equivalent form: gss24 |> tab(happy). We use direct calls when a single operation is enough. A named argument such as .by = sex sets an option; the = here supplies an argument value rather than saving an object. Grouped percentages are explained where they first appear.

After installing the packages (see Section 1.3), load them at the start of your session:

library(tidyverse)
library(tidystata)

We now move from country records to individual survey respondents, using one survey extract throughout this chapter and the following numerical-summary lessons. The 2024 General Social Survey (GSS) records adults’ demographic characteristics and answers about their families, work, education, and attitudes. The GSS has interviewed adults in the United States since 1972.

The one-time recipe in Appendix A explains how to obtain the freely available source data, prepare the variables, and save the gss24 extract as gss24.rds. Complete that recipe before running the examples below. If that file is in your working directory, load it with:

gss24 <- readRDS("gss24.rds")

readRDS() restores the saved R object, and <- stores it under the name gss24. You only need to load it once per session. Each row is one respondent; id is the respondent identifier. A row position or printed line number is not a permanent identity. Unknown answers remain missing rather than becoming response categories. The appendix prepares categorical columns with defined levels so we can tabulate them here without constructing factors ourselves.

Our extract contains 3,986 respondents and 25 variables. The main examples use full gss24. A final application creates a working-age subset and identifies its different percentage denominators explicitly.

8.1 Read frequency tables with tab(), tab1(), and tab2()

8.1.1 Start with numeric frequencies

Frequency tables help us inspect numeric measurements. Are recorded values repeated, and how far apart are the low and high values? In gss24, age records respondents’ ages in years. It measures a quantity rather than a named category, but tab() can still count every distinct recorded value. Store that result so we can inspect it in two ways:

age_freq <- tab(gss24, age)

Assignment with <- saves the full table under age_freq without printing it. Read the values and counts first; the next examples explain the percentage columns. Each row represents an exact recorded age, or a missing value, and n counts respondents with that value. These rows are not age brackets. The counts describe stored values: ages recorded in whole years are shared by many respondents.

There are many distinct values, so use the complementary previews from Section 4.4: head/tail to inspect the boundaries, and interval to inspect positions across the interior. Start with print_headtail():

The row numbers show where those ages occur in the complete table. The selected positions are evenly spaced through distinct recorded values, not through individual respondents. This preview does not produce age brackets, population quantiles, or a random sample. Repeated ages represent multiple respondents, and the missing category still comes last.

Both printing helpers return the entire age_freq table unchanged and invisibly, meaning that R displays the preview without automatically printing the returned object again. They do not remove rows, combine values into bins, or recalculate percentages. In particular, cum and valid_cum still include the categories skipped in the display; adding only the displayed percentages does not reconstruct the distribution. Exact-value frequencies are useful for inspection, while the numerical summaries in Chapter 9 give more compact descriptions of location and spread.

8.1.2 Count response categories

The GSS asks respondents how happy they are, all things considered: very happy, pretty happy, or not too happy. Which answer is most common? Tidystata’s tab() takes a table and the column to describe, returning one row per response category. Start by reading n, the number of respondents in each category:

tab(gss24, happy)
# A tibble: 4 × 6
  happy             n percent   cum valid valid_cum
  <fct>         <int>   <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>     <dbl>
1 very happy      797  20.0    20.0  20.2      20.2
2 pretty happy   2303  57.8    77.8  58.2      78.4
3 not too happy   854  21.4    99.2  21.6     100  
4 NA               32   0.803 100    NA        NA

Each output row represents a category rather than a respondent. The most common answer is “pretty happy,” with 2,303 respondents. The last row, <NA>, counts the 32 respondents with no recorded answer; they are still part of the dataset. Adding n across all rows gives the original 3,986 respondents.

The result is a new tibble; gss24 is unchanged. Counts tell us how many people gave each answer. The other columns express their relative sizes and make the denominator visible.

8.1.3 Read percentages

Besides the count n, tab() reports:

  • percent: the percentage of all respondents in each category.
  • cum: the running total of percent down the displayed rows.
  • valid: the percentage among respondents with a recorded answer, excluding missing answers from the denominator.
  • valid_cum: the running total of valid across the non-missing categories.

For “pretty happy,” percent divides its count by all 3,986 respondents, while valid divides by the 3,954 who answered. The valid percentage is 58.2%: more than half of those with a recorded answer chose this category. Missing answers have a count and an all-case percentage, but no valid percentage of their own.

Read cumulative percentages in the displayed category order. At “pretty happy,” valid_cum combines “very happy” and “pretty happy”: 78.4% of valid answers fall in those first two categories. Cumulative percentages depend on order and are useful only when combining successive categories answers a meaningful question. They are usually less informative for unordered categories such as marital status.

The appendix prepares happy as a factor, a column with a defined set of category levels. Those levels retain the source-code order: “very happy,” “pretty happy,” then “not too happy.” That is why these categories appear in this order rather than alphabetically. Cumulative totals follow that level order; preparation has not ranked respondents by how happy they are.

Because only 32 happiness answers are missing, percent and valid differ slightly. Section 8.2 returns to the choice of denominator.

8.1.4 Questions not asked of everyone

An unavailable answer need not be a refusal. The GSS uses ballots, different versions of the questionnaire assigned to parts of the sample. The institutional-confidence questions were asked on only two of the 2024 ballots. We therefore expect substantial missingness in conpress, confidence in the press:

tab(gss24, conpress)
# A tibble: 4 × 6
  conpress         n percent    cum valid valid_cum
  <fct>        <int>   <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl>     <dbl>
1 a great deal   225    5.64   5.64  8.68      8.68
2 only some     1045   26.2   31.9  40.3      49.0 
3 hardly any    1321   33.1   65.0  51.0     100   
4 NA            1395   35.0  100    NA        NA

The missing row counts about a third of the sample. percent includes all respondents, including people never assigned this question, in its denominator. valid describes the distribution among people with recorded confidence answers. That denominator excludes missing answers both by design and for other reasons; it is not the number of everyone who was assigned the question. A frequency table alone cannot distinguish these reasons. Consult the questionnaire and source documentation before describing missingness as refusal or lack of confidence.

To compare the counts across health groups more easily, arrange the same combinations as a contingency table with tab2():

tab2(gss24, happy, health)

Happiness categories form the rows and health categories the columns. Each cell is a count, not a percentage. For example, 246 respondents are very happy and report excellent health. The NA row and column retain missing answers; the bottom-right cell counts respondents missing both answers.

For a comparison within excellent health, divide the very-happy count by the sum of the three non-missing happiness counts in that column: 246 / (246 + 323 + 94). This gives about 37%. The corresponding share among respondents in poor health is 9%. These percentages describe those with recorded happiness answers in each health group; they are calculated from the counts, not printed by tab2() above. They show an association in this sample, not that health causes happiness. Section 8.2 shows how to request within-group percentages directly.

8.1.6 Tabulate several variables separately

Sometimes we want a separate distribution for each of several questions, rather than a table of their combinations. tab1(gss24, happy, health, marital) produces three one-way tables:

By default, the result is a named list: one object containing three separate tables. Its printed headings, $happy, $health, and $marital, identify those tables. Within each, value holds that variable’s categories; the remaining columns have the same meanings as in tab(). Each table has its own denominator, so do not add counts across the three tables: the same respondents contribute to each distribution.

For a single rectangular result, set .type = "tibble":

This argument changes the output format, not the calculation. The tables are stacked into one tibble, and the variable column identifies which question each row describes. Read variable and value together: “good” is a health answer, whereas “married” is a marital-status answer. Each row is now a category within a particular variable, rather than a combination of happiness, health, and marital status.

The happiness rows have exactly the same counts and percentages as the $happy table above; the same holds for health and marital status. Within each block, percent adds to 100 across all categories, and valid adds to 100 across non-missing answers. Each block’s counts add to 3,986, so summing n over the whole stacked result would count the same respondents three times. Stacking makes several distributions convenient to inspect together; it does not turn them into one distribution with a shared denominator.

8.1.7 Cross-tabulate several variable pairs

How are happiness, health, and marital status related to one another? We can inspect all three pairs without writing three separate calls. Give tab2() the same variables we just passed to tab1() and save its result. Typing its name displays the list at the normal console width:

pair_tables <- tab2(gss24, happy, health, marital)
pair_tables

The result is a list of three cross-tabulations. A narrow display may omit columns and name them in its footer; those columns remain in the returned tables. Inspect the result in your editor’s viewer when necessary. The list names identify the pairs:

  • happy x health: happiness in the rows and health in the columns, repeating our earlier two-variable table.
  • happy x marital: happiness in the rows and marital status in the columns.
  • health x marital: health in the rows and marital status in the columns.

Each cell counts respondents with that pair of answers, and NA rows or columns retain missing answers. Each table uses only its two named variables: someone with missing marital status can still contribute to a recorded happiness-health combination in the first table. Across all cells, including missing categories, each table counts the full sample once.

In the happiness-marital-status table, very-happy answers are more common among married respondents than among those who have never married. Divide the very-happy count in each column by that column’s total across the three recorded happiness categories. The resulting shares are 28.7% for married respondents and 13.0% for never-married respondents. These denominators exclude missing happiness answers within each marital-status group. The comparison describes an association, not an effect of marriage on happiness.

The three functions answer different questions even when given the same variables. tab1(gss24, happy, health, marital) describes each variable separately. tab2(gss24, happy, health, marital) describes each pair separately. By contrast, tab(gss24, happy, health, marital) would count joint combinations of all three, such as very happy, excellent health, and married. Three pairwise tables do not show how the happiness-health relationship varies within marital-status groups; that requires an additional grouping step, planned for a later chapter beyond this chapter.

8.1.8 Include totals with fre()

fre() works like tab() but adds total rows. These make the two denominators visible in the output:

fre(gss24, happy)
# A tibble: 6 × 6
  happy             n percent   cum valid valid_cum
  <chr>         <int>   <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>     <dbl>
1 very happy      797  20.0    20.0  20.2      20.2
2 pretty happy   2303  57.8    77.8  58.2      78.4
3 not too happy   854  21.4    99.2  21.6     100  
4 Valid Total    3954  99.2    NA   100        NA  
5 NA               32   0.803 100    NA        NA  
6 Total          3986 100      NA    NA        NA

Valid Total counts the 3,954 non-missing answers; Total counts all 3,986 respondents. The missing-answer count bridges the difference. These are summary rows, not additional response categories or additional respondents.

8.1.9 Optional reference: preserve the input table class

You can continue using tibbles throughout this chapter. If you work with other table classes, tidystata normally returns newly calculated tables in the same container as the input: a base data.frame, tibble, or data.table. This preserves the kind of table, not its original rows or columns.

as.data.frame() converts our tibble to a base data frame. Save the converted table separately:

gss_base <- as.data.frame(gss24)

class() takes an object and reports its class names. Compare the same tabulation with the two inputs:

class(tab(gss24, happy))
[1] "tbl_df"     "tbl"        "data.frame"
class(tab(gss_base, happy))
[1] "data.frame"

The tibble result has classes "tbl_df", "tbl", and "data.frame"; the base result has class "data.frame". A tibble is also a kind of data frame, which is why its class listing contains all three names. The category counts and percentages agree; only the container differs.

To try a data.table input, install the optional package once if needed:

install.packages("data.table")

data.table::as.data.table() converts the input without attaching the package. The :: notation names the package that supplies the function:

gss_dt <- data.table::as.data.table(gss24)

class(tab(gss_dt, happy))
[1] "data.table" "data.frame"

The result has classes "data.table" and "data.frame", so a data.table input produces a data.table frequency table. These calls return new results without changing gss24 or its converted copies.

For functions that return several tables, distinguish the outer list from the tables inside it. Store the default tab1() result as tables_dt. Double brackets, [[1]], extract the first table from that list:

tables_dt <- tab1(gss_dt, happy, health, marital)

class(tables_dt)
[1] "list"
class(tables_dt[[1]])
[1] "data.table" "data.frame"

The outer object is a "list"; its first table is a data.table. The other two tables preserve the same class. The three-variable tab2() result follows this pattern too: a list of cross-tabulations, each in the input’s table class.

An explicit output request can override this default. As we saw above, .type = "tibble" in tab1() both stacks the one-way tables and requests a tibble, including when the input is a data.table:

class(tab1(gss_dt, happy, health, marital, .type = "tibble"))
[1] "tbl_df"     "tbl"        "data.frame"

The class names are now "tbl_df", "tbl", and "data.frame". The distributions remain the same. When deciding how to use a result, check both its structure, one table or a list, and the class of the tables it contains.

8.2 Application: educational qualifications in the 2024 GSS

This section puts the tools together to describe education in the 2024 GSS, and shows why every percentage needs a clearly stated denominator.

8.2.1 Who has which degree?

What is the distribution of respondents’ highest educational qualifications? Each category of degree represents a highest degree, so someone with a graduate degree is not counted again under bachelor’s or high school:

tab(gss24, degree)
# A tibble: 6 × 6
  degree                       n percent    cum valid valid_cum
  <fct>                    <int>   <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl>     <dbl>
1 less than high school      362   9.08    9.08  9.10      9.10
2 high school               1817  45.6    54.7  45.7      54.8 
3 associate/junior college   360   9.03   63.7   9.05     63.8 
4 bachelor's                 863  21.7    85.3  21.7      85.5 
5 graduate                   577  14.5    99.8  14.5     100   
6 NA                           7   0.176 100    NA        NA

The last row shows that 7 respondents did not report a degree. That is why the two kinds of percentages differ:

  • percent divides by all 3,986 respondents.
  • valid divides by the 3,979 respondents who answered.

Here the difference is tiny. Among those who answered, 45.7% have a high school diploma as their highest degree, and 36.2% hold a bachelor’s or graduate degree.

For questions with more missing answers, the choice of denominator matters a great deal (see Exercise 2).

8.2.2 Comparing groups: which denominator?

To compare women and men, we need a distribution of degrees within each category of sex. The argument .by = sex asks tab() to perform its calculation separately for those groups, for this operation only.

tab() retains missing values, including a separate group for the 30 respondents whose sex is missing. This does not change the denominators within the recorded male and female groups. First save and display the returned table:

8.4 Chapter review

  • tab() counts records by category and reports percentages, retaining missing answers.
  • percent uses all observations and valid uses complete answers; fre() adds explicit total rows.
  • tab1() tabulates variables separately; tab2() produces a count matrix for each selected pair.
  • .by requests within-group distributions; check the grouping and missingness rules before interpreting a percentage.
  • print_headtail() and print_interval() preview a long frequency table without changing its contents or recalculating percentages.
  • Optional: class() checks the returned container; supported input classes are preserved by default, while .type can request tibbles explicitly.

Always identify a percentage’s denominator. Next, Chapter 9 describes numerical center and spread.
