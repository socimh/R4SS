3  Tabulation and summary statistics

NoteDraft chapter

This is a draft of Chapter 10 of the planned book. Until the intermediate chapters are revised, it appears directly after Chapter 2, so its chapter number will change.

TipIn this chapter
  • Count categories with count(), and add percentages with tab(), tab1(), tab2(), and fre().
  • Compute a single statistic with summarise(), or many statistics at once with summ().
  • Distinguish percentages of all cases from percentages of valid answers.
  • Describe education and income in the 2024 General Social Survey.

This chapter introduces two families of tools:

  • Categorical variables
    • count() counts the number of cases in each category.
    • tab() adds percentages and cumulative percentages.
    • tab1() produces these statistics for several variables at once.
    • tab2() cross-tabulates two variables.
  • Continuous variables
    • summarise() computes individual statistics.
    • summ() quickly produces many statistics for many variables.

count() and summarise() come from dplyr. tab(), tab1(), tab2(), and summ() come from tidystata, a package I wrote that brings several of Stata’s summary commands into tidyverse workflows.

The examples use two datasets:

  • gss24 comes from the 2024 General Social Survey (GSS), which has interviewed adults in the United States since 1972 about their families, work, education, and attitudes. Our extract has 3,986 respondents and 25 variables. Appendix A shows how to create it from freely available data.
  • lifeexp, included with tidystata, describes 68 countries in Europe and the Americas in 1998: life expectancy, population growth, income per person, and access to safe water. The package rebuilds this teaching dataset from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators, following the layout of Stata’s example. Most indicators refer to 1998; safe water refers to 2000.

3.1 count()

The GSS asks respondents how happy they are, all things considered: very happy, pretty happy, or not too happy.

library(tidyverse)

# Show the number of respondents in each category
gss24 |>
  count(happy)
# A tibble: 4 × 2
  happy             n
  <fct>         <int>
1 very happy      797
2 pretty happy   2303
3 not too happy   854
4 <NA>             32

The last row, <NA>, counts the 32 respondents who did not answer.

Besides the count n, tab() reports:

  • percent: the percentage of all respondents in each category.
  • cum: the cumulative percentage.
  • valid and valid_cum: the same percentages among respondents who answered.

3.3 summarise()

For continuous variables, we turn to lifeexp. The variable lexp is life expectancy at birth, in years.

# Mean life expectancy across countries
lifeexp |>
  summarise(mean(lexp))
# A tibble: 1 × 1
  `mean(lexp)`
         <dbl>
1         71.7
# Name the result
lifeexp |>
  summarise(lexp = mean(lexp))
# A tibble: 1 × 1
   lexp
  <dbl>
1  71.7

Each statistic needs its own expression. Describing a single variable fully soon becomes long:

lifeexp |>
  summarise(
    min = min(lexp),
    q1 = quantile(lexp, .25),
    mean = mean(lexp),
    median = median(lexp),
    sd = sd(lexp),
    q3 = quantile(lexp, .75),
    max = max(lexp)
  )
# A tibble: 1 × 7
    min    q1  mean median    sd    q3   max
  <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1    57  68.8  71.7     72  5.16    76    79

3.4 summ()

summarise() is a basic building block. To make everyday descriptions quicker without giving up flexibility, I designed summ(). Here are its basic features:

# The type, number of non-missing values, number of distinct values,
# minimum, mean, standard deviation, and maximum of life expectancy
lifeexp |>
  summ(lexp)
# A tibble: 1 × 7
  type      n unique   min  mean    sd   max
  <chr> <int>  <int> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1 dbl      68     20    57  71.7  5.16    79

The statistics above are:

  • type: the type of the variable (or its unit, if the variable has one).
  • n: the number of non-missing values.
  • unique: the number of distinct values.
    • Clearly, unique \(\leq\) n.
    • A larger unique means the variable is more “continuous” and carries more information.
    • For example, age in years is usually a whole number between 0 and 120. Age in months has 12 times as many possible values, and age in days even more. Of the three, age in days is the smoothest and most continuous, carries the most information, and has the largest unique.
    • Here, life expectancy is recorded in whole years, so 68 countries share only 20 distinct values. Survey and administrative data are often like this: many variables are counts, categories, or rounded numbers.
  • min: the minimum.
  • mean: the mean.
  • sd: the standard deviation.
  • max: the maximum.

When you summarize several variables, a name column identifies each one:

# Summarize several variables at once
lifeexp |>
  summ(popgrowth:safewater)
# A tibble: 4 × 8
  name      type      n unique   min     mean       sd     max

The detailed statistics describe the shape of each distribution:

summ() uses Stata’s percentile rule, so its quartiles can differ from those produced by R’s default quantile().

  • For gnppc, the mean ($9,137) is far above the median ($3,380), and the skewness (skew) is positive. A few rich countries, such as Switzerland and Norway, pull the mean up, as income distributions usually do. The median better describes a typical country.
  • For lexp, the skewness is negative: most countries cluster in the high 60s and 70s, while a few, such as Haiti at 57 years, have much lower life expectancy.
# Choose your own statistics
lifeexp |>
  summ(popgrowth:safewater, .stat = c("valid_pct", "mean", "se"))
# A tibble: 4 × 5

valid_pct is the proportion of non-missing values, and se is the standard error, an estimate of the uncertainty in the mean.

Only 79.4% of countries report safewater, and the missing values are not spread evenly: 12 of the 14 countries without data are in the Americas. The mean of 77.2% describes the countries with reported data, most of which are in Europe and Central Asia. It does not summarize all 68 countries. Always check who is missing before interpreting a mean.

The messages and the result show how summ() treats different kinds of variables:

  • Cannot be summarized: text, such as string. summ() drops it with a message; use tab1() instead. An entirely missing variable, such as all_miss, is kept with n = 0.
  • Can be summarized, but the results are usually not meaningful: factors, such as factor and order, marked with **. Use tab1() instead.
  • Can be summarized meaningfully: numbers, numbers with units, durations, and logical (dummy) variables.
  • Summarized in their own tables: dates, date-times, and times of day. Their means and ranges are displayed as formatted calendar dates and times, while standard deviations are durations. By default, summ() returns a list with one table for each kind.

3.5 Application: education and income in the 2024 GSS

This section puts the tools together to describe education in the 2024 GSS, and shows why every percentage needs a clearly stated denominator.

3.5.1 Who has which degree?

gss24 |>
  tab(degree)
# A tibble: 6 × 6
  degree                       n percent    cum valid valid_cum
  <fct>                    <int>   <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl>     <dbl>
1 less than high school      362   9.08    9.08  9.10      9.10
2 high school               1817  45.6    54.7  45.7      54.8 
3 associate/junior college   360   9.03   63.7   9.05     63.8 
4 bachelor's                 863  21.7    85.3  21.7      85.5 
5 graduate                   577  14.5    99.8  14.5     100   
6 <NA>                         7   0.176 100    NA        NA

The last row shows that 7 respondents did not report a degree. That is why the two kinds of percentages differ:

  • percent divides by all 3,986 respondents.
  • valid divides by the 3,979 respondents who answered.

Here the difference is tiny. Among those who answered, 45.7% have a high school diploma as their highest degree, and 36.2% hold a bachelor’s or graduate degree.

For questions with more missing answers, the choice of denominator matters a great deal (see Exercise 2).

3.5.3 Age, schooling, and income

gss24 |>
  summ(age, educ, childs, realinc)
# A tibble: 4 × 8
  name    type      n unique   min     mean       sd     max
  <chr>   <chr> <int>  <int> <dbl>    <dbl>    <dbl>   <dbl>
1 age     dbl    3859     72   18     49.0     17.7      89 
2 educ    dbl    3952     21    0     14.2      2.92     20 
3 childs  dbl    3944      9    0      1.72     1.65      8 
4 realinc dbl    3563     26  182. 34252.   34697.   117565.
  • age is in years.
  • educ is the number of years of schooling completed.
  • childs is the number of children the respondent has ever had.
  • realinc is family income in constant 1986 dollars. The GSS asks about income in brackets, so realinc has only 26 distinct values.

Each row uses the respondents who answered that question, so n differs across variables.

To compare income across educational groups, tabstat(), a companion of summ() named after the Stata command, places selected statistics side by side:

gss24 |>
  filter(!is.na(degree)) |>
  tabstat(realinc, .by = degree, .stat = c("n", "mean", "median"))
# A tibble: 5 × 5
  degree                   type      n   mean median
  <fct>                    <chr> <int>  <dbl>  <dbl>
1 less than high school    dbl     287 13210.  7714.
2 high school              dbl    1621 24308. 16335 
3 associate/junior college dbl     336 31510. 19965 
4 bachelor's               dbl     783 46575. 36300 
5 graduate                 dbl     530 59746. 43560

Median family income rises at every step of education, from $7,714 among respondents without a high school diploma to $43,560 among those with a graduate degree. In every group, the mean is above the median, a sign that a few high incomes pull the mean up.

Two cautions apply to these descriptions:

  • They describe associations in a sample. They do not show that a degree causes higher income.
  • They are unweighted sample statistics. The GSS provides survey weights for estimating population figures; we return to weights later in the book.

3. Diagnose the code. The following code returns NA. Why, and how would you fix it?

gss24 |>

TipAnswer

age is missing for 127 respondents, and in R any calculation involving a missing value returns NA unless you say otherwise. Add na.rm = TRUE, or use summ(), which reports statistics for the non-missing values together with their number n:

gss24 |>
  summarise(mean_age = mean(age, na.rm = TRUE))
