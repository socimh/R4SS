26  Fixed effects

Does the association between parent and respondent schooling persist when we compare respondents within the same region? The Chapter 24 chapter adjusted for age and sex. We now let regions have different intercepts while retaining a common parental-schooling slope. A second application uses repeated observations of the same people to explain what changes when the group is a person rather than a region.

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Fit and interpret a linear model with group fixed effects.
  • Compare specifications on the same observations and check equivalent slope estimates.
  • Verify the structure and within-person variation of a panel dataset.
  • Fit person and year fixed effects with person-clustered uncertainty.
  • Explain the limits of fixed effects, including absorbed variables and causal claims.

Use the table operations from earlier chapters, factors from Chapter 15, and formulas and coefficient tables from Chapter 24. Prepare gss24.rds using the Appendix A and keep it in your working directory. The setup below is complete for a fresh session. Package prefixes such as fixest:: identify a function’s package without attaching it.

The new fitting package is fixest. The later panel application also uses data included in plm. Install both once, rather than every time you run the chapter. This installation chunk is deliberately not evaluated when the book renders.

install.packages(c("fixest", "plm"))
library(tidyverse)
library(tidystata)
gss24 <- readRDS("gss24.rds")

26.1 Compare within regions

The main outcome remains educ, the highest year of school completed. maeduc records mother’s schooling or younger-parent schooling for same-sex parents, following the source definition. Age and sex retain their earlier roles. The new variable, region, identifies the respondent’s current region. It does not necessarily describe where the respondent grew up or attended school.

A group fixed effect gives each group its own intercept. Respondents in the same region share that region’s intercept, while each respondent retains their own predictor values. In our additive model, the parent-schooling, age, and sex coefficients are shared across regions. Different intercepts do not imply different slopes. An interaction would be needed to let the parent-schooling slope vary by region; that is a different model from the one fitted here.

For this GSS comparison, fixed effects are a way to include categorical region adjustment in a linear model. They do not turn the GSS into a panel. Each row is still a different respondent observed once. The later application will use repeated observations and person fixed effects, where the available comparisons change more substantially.

First reconstruct the preceding OLS and logit chapters’ complete-case sample, adding the requirement that region is known. We retain degree even though this chapter does not model it, so the comparison preserves the earlier sample definition. Both models below use the resulting table without further filtering. Male remains the reference sex; converting region to a factor ensures that region codes are treated as categories rather than a numeric trend.

region_data <- gss24 |>
  select(id, educ, maeduc, age, sex, degree, region) |>
  filter(!is.na(id), !is.na(educ), !is.na(maeduc), !is.na(age),
         !is.na(sex), !is.na(degree), !is.na(region)) |>
  mutate(
    sex = factor(sex, levels = c("male", "female")),
    region = factor(region)
  )

The analysis retains 3329 respondents. The frequency table counts respondents within each of the four recorded regions; it is not a count of repeated observations of people. The numerical summary describes schooling and age in years. These checks establish the sample and units before a model comparison. Excluding unknown values makes the comparison consistent, but does not establish that retained respondents resemble those excluded.

The GSS models in this chapter are unweighted teaching examples with ordinary model-based uncertainty. They do not account for the survey’s sampling design and do not provide survey-adjusted population estimates. The available survey weight is not a substitute for specifying that design. Nor would clustering on only four broad regions provide a reliable general solution to uncertainty here.

26.1.1 Include region indicators

We can fit region fixed effects with the familiar stats::lm(). Add region to the formula alongside the other explanatory terms. R represents the region factor using an omitted reference region and indicators for the other regions. In this sample, northeast is the reference region. Each displayed region coefficient is an intercept difference from the reference, conditional on the other modeled terms.

region_adjusted <- stats::lm(
  educ ~ maeduc + age + sex, data = region_data
)
region_lm <- stats::lm(
  educ ~ maeduc + age + sex + region, data = region_data
)

The output retains the familiar maeduc, age, and sexfemale coefficients, followed by the region contrasts. The omitted region has no separate coefficient because its intercept is already represented by (Intercept). A positive region contrast means higher fitted schooling than the reference region at the same modeled parent schooling, age, and sex. It is not a claim that moving there would increase schooling. Current residence may itself reflect earlier educational and employment choices.

Both models use 3329 observations. To focus on our question, make one coefficient table containing just the parental-schooling rows. bind_rows() stacks tables that have matching column names. Its named inputs become values in the new model column when we supply .id = "model". The familiar tidy() supplies coefficient tables; filter() and select() retain the comparison we want.

region_comparison <- bind_rows(

  "Age, sex, and region" = broom::tidy(region_lm, conf.int = TRUE),
  .id = "model"

Each row is an estimate from a model, rather than a respondent. The columns contain the parental-schooling slope, its standard error, and the endpoints of its ordinary 95% confidence interval. The slope unit is respondent-schooling years per parent-schooling year.

knitr::kable() formats this small table for reading. digits = 3 rounds displayed numeric values to three decimals without changing the stored estimates. The caption identifies the sample and uncertainty; the automatic table number can be used in the text.

knitr::kable(region_comparison, digits = 3)

The parental-schooling association is essentially unchanged after allowing region intercepts. In Table 26.1, both estimates round to 0.299 additional years of respondent schooling per additional year of parent schooling. More precisely, they are 0.299025 without region and 0.299164 with region. We should report this small change rather than expect fixed effects to produce a dramatic revision.

The region-adjusted slope describes a shared within-region association, conditional on modeled age and sex. It does not hold every family circumstance constant. Broad current regions leave substantial differences within regions, and do not measure childhood institutions directly. A nearly unchanged coefficient neither proves that geography is unimportant nor establishes that the parental-schooling association is causal.

26.1.2 Absorb the intercepts

For a few groups, displaying their indicators is manageable. With hundreds of people or organizations, the group intercepts can crowd out the coefficients of interest. fixest::feols() fits a linear fixed-effects model while absorbing those intercepts: it accounts for them without listing every one among the reported slope coefficients.

In its formula, the single vertical bar separates ordinary explanatory terms from the fixed-effect grouping variable. educ ~ maeduc + age + sex | region keeps schooling as the outcome, puts the three explanatory variables before |, and places region after it. This formula separator is different from the native pipe |>, which passes an object into a function. vcov = "iid" requests ordinary independent, constant-variance model-based uncertainty for this GSS demonstration.

region_fe <- fixest::feols(
  educ ~ maeduc + age + sex | region,
  data = region_data, vcov = "iid"
)

The printed model identifies region as a fixed effect and reports the nonabsorbed coefficients. The parent-schooling estimate should match the lm() model that included the region factor. Absorption changes how we obtain and present that estimate, not the underlying additive comparison.

Use the earlier coefficient-table tools to inspect that equivalence. The maeduc rows should have the same estimate apart from numerical rounding. Check the shared observation count as well. Exact reporting conventions for degrees of freedom or fit measures can differ across software; matching slope estimates is the equivalence demonstrated here.

region_equivalence <- bind_rows(
  "lm indicators" = broom::tidy(region_lm),
  "feols absorption" = broom::tidy(region_fe),
  .id = "method"
) |>
  filter(term == "maeduc") |>
  select(method, estimate)

Both implementations use the same respondents and produce the same parental-schooling slope. The fixed-effect model has not estimated a separate parental-schooling slope for every region. It has estimated a common slope while permitting the baseline level of schooling to differ across regions.

NoteOptional: subtract the region means

A bounded numerical demonstration helps explain “within region.” For this demonstration only, omit age and sex and compare a simpler region-adjusted model with a model of deviations from region means. This is not a reconstruction of the full adjusted model above.

Inside mutate(), .by = region calculates each mean within a region and repeats that mean for its members. Subtracting it gives a respondent’s distance above or below the region mean. We create two new columns in a separate table, preserving the original schooling values.

region_centered <- region_data |>
  mutate(
    educ_within = educ - mean(educ),
    maeduc_within = maeduc - mean(maeduc),
    .by = region
  )

26.2 Secondary application: a panel

How are wages associated with union membership and marriage when we follow the same people over time? The GSS extract does not follow respondents annually, so this question requires a different dataset. We now use Males, a historical panel supplied by plm. Keeping the application together makes the dataset change explicit: the unit becomes one person in one year, and the outcome becomes the logarithm of hourly wages.

The installed plm help entry for Males describes observations of 545 young men over 1980–1987, with eight annual observations per person. Its source is the wage-panel application associated with Vella and Verbeek (1998), “Whose wages do unions raise? A dynamic model of unionism and wage rate determination for young men,” Journal of Applied Econometrics, 13, 163–183. This restricted historical sample should not be described as all workers or as a contemporary national workforce.

Record the package versions alongside this source reference. utils::packageVersion() takes a quoted package name and returns its installed version. The first result identifies the data package; the second identifies the fixed-effects implementation used for this application. Recording versions helps distinguish this particular reproducible example from later package updates.

utils::packageVersion("plm")
[1] '2.6.7'
utils::packageVersion("fixest")
[1] '0.14.1'

utils::data() loads a dataset distributed inside an installed package. Its first argument names the dataset and package = "plm" identifies its source. It creates Males in the session; no external file download is needed for the lesson.

utils::data("Males", package = "plm")

An empty result means every observed combination is unique; it does not yet establish that every expected year exists for every person.

Zero rows have missing values in these core variables. Both wage models can therefore use all 4,360 rows. This check concerns recorded values; it does not assess measurement accuracy or how these people entered the source panel.

26.2.2 Find within-person variation

A person fixed effect permits every person to have a different intercept. The explanatory slopes then use changes within people over time. A variable that never changes for a person cannot explain that person’s deviations from their own level.

n_distinct() counts distinct observed values. Inside summarise() with .by = person_id, it tells us how many union states, marital states, or schooling values each person has across their eight records. Because the core status variables are complete, one state means no recorded change and two states means at least one yes/no change.

panel_variation <- panel_data |>
  summarise(
    union_states = n_distinct(union),
    married_states = n_distinct(married),
    school_values = n_distinct(school),
    .by = person_id
  )
count(panel_variation, union_states)
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  union_states     n
         <int> <int>
1            1   299
2            2   246

count(panel_variation, married_states)
# A tibble: 2 × 2
  married_states     n
           <int> <int>
1              1   235
2              2   310

count(panel_variation, school_values)
# A tibble: 1 × 2
  school_values     n
          <int> <int>
1             1   545

The first two results distinguish people who change union or marital status from those whose recorded status is constant. The last shows one schooling value for all 545 people. Person fixed effects therefore absorb schooling in this dataset: there is no within-person schooling change from which to estimate a separate schooling slope. Source ethnicity is also time invariant and is absorbed.

“Absorbed” does not mean that schooling or ethnicity is substantively unimportant. Their stable association with each person’s wage level cannot be separated from that person’s unrestricted intercept in this specification. Similarly, linear experience increases with calendar time within each person. Its linear term is collinear with person and year effects here, so we do not add it to the two-way model. Estimating a coefficient requires variation that remains after all included fixed effects are accounted for.

26.2.3 Fit person and year effects

First fit a pooled wage model with year fixed effects. It compares records across different people as well as within people, while allowing a common intercept shift for every calendar year. The union and married factors use no as their reference categories; unionyes and marriedyes are yes-minus-no contrasts. Check these categories using the familiar frequency tables before fitting.

For panel uncertainty, vcov = ~ person_id requests standard errors clustered by person. The one-sided formula names the cluster identifier; it does not add a predictor. This allows errors from different years for the same person to be dependent, while treating different people as independent clusters. It changes the uncertainty calculation, not the fitted slope coefficients. We have 545 person clusters, unlike the four regions in the GSS example.

panel_year <- fixest::feols(
  log_wage ~ union + married | year,
  data = panel_data, vcov = ~ person_id
)

Now put person_id + year after the formula separator. The plus sign includes both sets of intercepts. Person fixed effects account for stable person-level differences; year effects account for changes shared across the sample in a given year. The union and marriage coefficients use the remaining within-person variation, after those year shifts are taken into account.

knitr::kable(panel_comparison, digits = 3)

The positive wage associations are smaller when we compare the same people across years. In Table 26.2, the union coefficient changes from about 0.176 to 0.083 log points, and the marriage coefficient from about 0.142 to 0.058 log points. The second model’s union coefficient describes the fitted difference between a person’s union and nonunion records, at the same modeled marital status and after accounting for common year shifts. The marriage coefficient has the corresponding interpretation at the same modeled union status.

The outcome is a natural logarithm, so these coefficients are differences in log hourly wages, called log points. They are not currency amounts or percentage-point differences. We keep that scale throughout this comparison rather than relabel the estimates as exact percentage changes. The interval columns also use log points and quantify model-based uncertainty with person clustering.

These within-person comparisons do not identify causal union or marriage effects on their own. Job changes, health, employment conditions, and other changing circumstances can affect both wages and the explanatory variables. People may enter a union job or marry partly in response to changing prospects. Person effects remove additive differences that stay constant, not changing omitted explanations. Year effects remove common time shifts, not every person’s particular trajectory. Clustering addresses dependence in uncertainty, not these identification problems.

26.3 Report the comparison

In the GSS application, the parental-schooling association is nearly unchanged after allowing current-region intercepts. It remains an unweighted conditional association among the same complete-case respondents, with no survey-design correction. Region fixed effects adjust levels across four groups, but neither reconstruct childhood context nor explain away differences within a region.

In the secondary panel application, union membership and marriage have smaller positive log-wage associations after adding person effects to the year effects. The contrast is informative because the records are unchanged and the comparison is explicit. Repeated observations permit us to separate stable person differences from within-person change. They do not ensure that changes in union membership or marriage occurred independently of other wage determinants.

Report the observation unit, sample, fixed-effect groups, coefficient scale, and uncertainty method together. “Fixed effects” alone does not tell readers whether the groups were regions, people, years, or another unit. It also does not state whether uncertainty was ordinary, clustered, or adjusted for a survey design.

26.4 Review

  • Group fixed effects give groups separate intercepts while keeping the specified slopes common.
  • lm() includes factor indicators; feols() can absorb their intercepts and recover equivalent slopes.
  • Keep the sample fixed when comparing specifications and check observation counts with nobs().
  • count() checks panel keys and waves; n_distinct() identifies available within-person variation.
  • Person effects absorb time-invariant variables; person and year effects can also absorb common linear trends.
  • State coefficient units and the uncertainty method. Fixed effects and clustered standard errors do not establish a causal interpretation.

The optional Programming part develops expressions and objects further. These models can already be reconstructed from the data preparation, formulas, and checks shown here.
